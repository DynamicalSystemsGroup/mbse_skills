# Adaptive engineering question patterns

Choose the next relevant gap, not the next row in a checklist. These are
original stems informed by the sources below; they are not an institutional
questionnaire or a requirement to ask all of them.

| Gap | Open question | Later narrowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | What problem should this system or model help resolve? | Which decision must the model support first? Offer alternatives grounded in the answer. |
| Experience | Walk me through a recent or representative operating scenario. | Where does the current approach fail, and what is observed at that point? |
| Boundary | What does the system control, and what must it rely on externally? | Clarify one ambiguous interface or ownership boundary. |
| Stakeholders | Who experiences the outcome, and whose concerns differ? | Identify who can resolve a specific competing obligation. |
| Rationale | What makes that condition important for the goal? | Ask for a source, consequence or counterexample if the rationale remains unclear. |
| Acceptance | What observation would convince you this worked? | Ask for the missing threshold, condition, units or evidence source without inventing values. |
| Tradeoff | Where do the goals pull in different directions? | Offer contextual alternatives and explain what each sacrifices; preserve the possibility of revising assumptions. |
| Robustness | In what situation would this approach stop being acceptable? | Examine the material exception, failure mode or uncertainty. |
| Domain research | What source-backed distinction do we need to establish? | Ask for specification edition/clause, example and limitation; avoid turning an unresolved fact into a poll. |

One row can require several turns, or none. Keep examples small enough to help
answering rather than adding a second assignment. An open question normally
has no recommended answer. Later choice prompts can look like:

> Which outcome matters first for this model?
>
> 1. Explain the requirement assumptions for review.
> 2. Evaluate the defined constraint for a supplied scenario.
> 3. Compare candidate designs against agreed criteria.
>
> You can describe a different outcome or defer the choice.

Recommend a choice only after its prerequisites are established. If a user
says “healthy reserve,” ask what observation defines healthy; do not offer 20%
as though it were an agreed requirement. If inputs reveal infeasible goals,
describe the conflict and ask about authority or negotiability instead of
quietly relaxing one goal.

## Source principles and transfer limits

- [GOV.UK in-depth interviews](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews): use a discussion guide flexibly, ask neutral open questions and seek concrete stories. We apply these interview practices to engineering discovery.
- [IDEO Design Kit Five Whys](https://www.designkit.org/methods/the-five-whys.html): explore the rationale beneath an answer. Our adaptation uses selective depth, not a mandatory chain of five questions.
- [Pew question design](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/): offered options, wording and order can influence responses. This survey evidence motivates caution about early suggested answers; it does not establish agent performance.
- [NASA stakeholder expectations](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/4-1-stakeholder-expectations-definition/): elicit needs, scenarios, stakeholders, assumptions and constraints; separate those from design and contractual requirements. NASA's project context needs tailoring.

No source establishes a universally correct conversation length. One main
question per turn, optional later choices and stopping at useful clarity are
this collection's conventions. No special service, script or hook is required.
